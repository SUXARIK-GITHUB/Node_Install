Soundings and depth

Depth soundings are meaningful only with chart datum and unit context.

Depth figures are referenced to a defined chart datum, not simply today's water surface. Check tides, chart corrections and the limits of old surveys.

Read a sample chart's depth soundings, reference datum and scale before drawing a shallow-water boundary. Never infer a safe route from decorative contours.

02 / Sailing

Reading a buoy

A buoy's shape and color are part of its navigation meaning.

Buoys mark channels or hazards through standardized color, shape and light characteristics. Their meaning depends on the local buoyage system.

Sketch a buoy with its shape, light and color, then compare it with an authoritative chart legend. A memory aid cannot replace current navigation notices.

03 / Sailing

The harbor plan

Harbor plans should be checked against current notices and conditions.

A harbor plan should show entries, restricted zones and places suitable for turning. It cannot replace updated notices or instruction from local authorities.

Create a harbor entrance diagram with traffic areas and published restrictions clearly labelled as examples. Real passage demands up-to-date official information.

04 / Sailing

A compass line

A compass line helps describe bearing but cannot replace situational awareness.

A compass course is influenced by variation and sometimes deviation. Distinguish map bearings from the heading shown on an instrument.

Draw true and magnetic direction as separate lines on a compass sketch, recording the source of any variation. Avoid applying an undated correction at sea.

What to look for: Reading a chart legend before plotting a route

Depth soundings and navigational marks have meaning only together with chart datum, scale and the publication's update history. A clean route line may cross hazards that are invisible in an old small-scale illustration. Treat this as a reading exercise; decisions afloat require current charts, local notices and an experienced navigator.

Field guide

Reading a chart legend before plotting a route

The symbols on a marine chart are tied to a particular edition and scale. A novice should read the legend before drawing conclusions.

Method

  1. Choose a current official teaching chart or legally available excerpt and record edition, projection and scale.
  2. Identify depth contours, restricted zones, navigation marks and the convention used for soundings without copying symbols into an invented map.
  3. Compare the chart to its authoritative correction notices and list which elements may be outdated.
