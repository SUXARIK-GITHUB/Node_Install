Objects as evidence

Objects become evidence when their materials and context are recorded.

Everyday objects can document how people solved a recurring problem. Study wear, construction and labels before attributing a design intention.

Write an object label with material, likely use and verified provenance in separate fields. Make uncertainty visible rather than turning a guess into an attribution.

Typography in time

Typography reveals the tools and conventions of its period.

Typography changes with available tools and reading habits. Compare specimens from similar periods to avoid turning one unusual example into a trend.

Compare two dated typographic samples from reliable collections, noting what actually changed in production. Do not invent an era from stylistic resemblance.

Material samples

Samples are more useful when their properties are clearly labeled.

Material samples make differences in weight, texture and finish tangible. Label each specimen clearly and describe how it was produced.

Arrange a few safe material swatches by texture and process and give each a precise caption. Visitor access and preservation may require different handling.

A small catalogue

A small catalogue needs consistent terms before beautiful typography.

A small catalogue should connect objects with dates, creators where known and source records. Mark uncertainty rather than inventing complete provenance.

Sketch a miniature catalogue spread with an index, object number and source note. Test how quickly a reader can find one item without a guide.

What to look for: A label for an everyday design object

An object label is most useful when it distinguishes observable material and construction from stories about the designer's intent. Compare a chair, package or printed page through use, wear and manufacture; avoid inventing provenance for an anonymous item. A small catalogue can be rigorous without pretending to own a historical collection.

Reading questions

  1. While reading “Objects as evidence”, look for observable evidence related to “Object”. Visible material and construction described. Compare it with “Typography in time”: which condition needs its own note before a conclusion is justified? What is directly visible, and what remains an interpretation?

  2. While reading “Typography in time”, look for observable evidence related to “Source”. Every historical claim has a citation. Compare it with “Material samples”: which condition needs its own note before a conclusion is justified? What is directly visible, and what remains an interpretation?

  3. While reading “Material samples”, look for observable evidence related to “Unknowns”. Missing attribution is explicit. Compare it with “A small catalogue”: which condition needs its own note before a conclusion is justified? What is directly visible, and what remains an interpretation?

Field guide

A label for an everyday design object

Museum-style text should help readers see material and function without inventing a designer, date or provenance.

Method

  1. Choose an object you own and record observable details: materials, dimensions, joints and signs of wear.
  2. Write an eighty-word label that separates the visible features from claims requiring catalog research.
  3. Add a note identifying where provenance could be checked and mark any unknown author or date as unknown.

Limits and safety

Do not reproduce protected catalog images without rights or invent a museum collection around an ordinary object.
