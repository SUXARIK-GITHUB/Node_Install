Stories

A good route begins long before the first step, with a map and a question about the terrain.

01

Before the trail

A planned route should account for daylight, elevation, terrain and an exit option. Share the plan with a trusted person before departure.

A route plan should include emergency contacts, a return time and an alternative exit. Read current advisories rather than relying on a saved screenshot.

02

Contours and slopes

Close contour lines mean a steeper climb. Reading the terrain from a map helps anticipate effort before a hike.

Contour spacing shows the steepness of mapped terrain, but surface conditions still matter. Combine map reading with local trail reports.

03

Weather windows

A pleasant forecast at the trailhead does not imply identical conditions at higher elevations. Check regional advisories.

Mountain forecasts may vary sharply by elevation and exposure. Check wind and precipitation as well as temperature before setting out.

04

Leave the landscape intact

Follow established paths where possible and bring waste back. Observe wildlife without approaching or feeding it.

A low-impact visit also means staying out of sensitive restoration areas. Treat closures and wildlife distances as part of the route plan.

What to look for: A route card for a modest day hike

A route description should expose its uncertainties: terrain, weather, alternative exits and which observations are dated. An elegant contour map does not guarantee the condition of a path on the day of travel. Avoid sharing sensitive wildlife locations and let safety decisions rest on current local sources rather than editorial sketches.

Field guide

A route card for a modest day hike

A good field route description tells the reader when not to go. The example uses no invented trail names or distances: replace placeholders only after checking a current local map.

Method

  1. Identify the start, destination and nearest safe exit. Confirm that the route is legally accessible, check elevation changes and note any stream crossings or exposed paths.
  2. Check current weather, warnings and daylight. Choose a return deadline with spare time, carry navigation independent of cellular data and tell someone the intended route.
  3. Afterward, record actual conditions, route changes and any confusing markings. A qualitative account is useful when it clearly distinguishes a personal observation from route data.
