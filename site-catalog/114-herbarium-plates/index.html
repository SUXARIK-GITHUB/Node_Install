Leaf architecture

Veins distribute water and strengthen the blade. Their branching patterns provide useful visual clues, though identification should rely on multiple features.

02

Seed dispersal

Wind, water, and animals move seeds in very different ways. Studying the surrounding habitat is as important as examining the seed itself.

03

Pressed specimens

Drying a specimen carefully preserves a record of its shape. Collection must follow local conservation rules and should avoid protected plants.

04

Seasonal change

The color of a leaf reflects changing pigments and environmental conditions. A dated sketch can communicate details that a single label misses.

Start here: A leaf record without removing a plant

A leaf plate can be drawn directly from observation, leaving the plant in place. The aim is to capture identifying structure and context rather than inventing a precise species from one image.

  1. From a permitted path, sketch the general outline and the way the veins divide. Compare one mature leaf with another on the same plant without damaging either.

  2. Record growth form, approximate leaf size, position on the stem, date and habitat. If photographing, include a wider view to retain the context of the plant.

  3. Consult a reliable regional field guide before assigning a name. Keep uncertainty on the page and avoid conclusions about edibility, toxicity or medicinal use from the sketch.

What to notice: Margin: Smooth, serrated or lobed edge, observed rather than assumed.; Venation: Visible branching of the main and side veins.; Context: Tree, shrub or herb and the surroundings in which it grows.

A pressed leaf preserves an outline but loses information about color, habitat and three-dimensional growth. A responsible field record pairs the specimen drawing with date and surroundings, without disturbing protected plants. Identification should use multiple traits; a stylized illustration alone is not a reliable species key.
