A quiet scale

A quiet scale teaches more when each note is heard distinctly.

A slow scale can reveal unevenness between fingers more clearly than a fast run. Work within a comfortable tempo rather than forcing speed.

Design a short practice sequence for one simple scale with a slow tempo and comfortable range. Avoid assigning a fixed speed as a measure of progress.

02 / Music

Listening to resonance

Resonance changes with pedal, instrument and the room.

Resonance changes when notes overlap and the damper pedal is used. Listen to how harmony clears when the pedal is released.

Listen to the decay of a single original piano note at different dynamics and describe what you hear. Room acoustics can change the impression.

03 / Music

Hands and posture

Relaxed posture is important before increasing practice duration.

Comfortable posture involves relaxed shoulders and an adjustable distance to the keys. Pain or persistent tension signals that technique needs attention.

Sketch neutral wrist and hand positions at the keyboard, leaving room for individual anatomy. Persistent pain is a reason to stop and seek guidance.

A patient practice

A short daily exercise is easier to observe and improve.

A regular short session can be more useful than an occasional long practice. Write down one passage to revisit and what improved.

Keep a practice log with duration, one technical focus and the next small question. Consistent notes can be more useful than a long unstructured session.

What to look for: A slow practice log for one short phrase

Good practice separates the movement being learned from the sound the player hopes to hear. Compare a short phrase slowly and at an ordinary tempo, listening for balance and unnecessary tension. Posture advice must respect individual bodies; stop at discomfort and seek a qualified teacher rather than forcing a fixed hand shape.

Reading questions

Field guide

A slow practice log for one short phrase

A practice diary is more useful than an unsupported claim of progress. This exercise documents one self-chosen musical phrase.

Method

  1. Select an original or public-domain four-measure phrase and mark the fingering you intend to try at a comfortable tempo.
  2. Play slowly with relaxed hands, noting only where rhythm or transitions become uneven instead of forcing speed.
  3. Repeat on another day and compare descriptions of the same difficulty without assigning invented accuracy percentages.
