Contact sheets

Contact sheets reveal a sequence that a single chosen photograph hides.

A contact sheet makes relationships between frames visible: focus, gesture and slight shifts in timing. Number frames so that choices remain traceable.

Arrange a contact sheet of your own practice frames and mark the exposures worth revisiting. Keep contact-sheet numbers consistent with the originals.

Quiet portraits

A calm portrait often depends more on patience than elaborate equipment.

A portrait can feel quiet because the subject has room to look outside the frame. Consider whether the background gives context or distracts.

Sketch a portrait composition that respects a person's consent and personal space. Use lighting diagrams, not invented people or unapproved photographs.

03 / Photography

Light direction

Side light gives texture to surfaces and faces.

Side light reveals relief and texture while frontal light reduces shadows. Compare the same object in both conditions before making a judgment.

Photograph the same harmless object under side and frontal light with unchanged framing. Describe shadow direction rather than attributing a mood as a fact.

Archiving negatives

Negative sleeves need careful labeling and dry storage.

Negatives need stable storage and reliable identification. Record film type, date and sequence, and avoid touching image surfaces with bare fingers.

Create a negative-storage label with frame number, date and storage conditions. Avoid keeping identifiable photographs in public folders without permission.

What to look for: A contact sheet that explains the selection

A contact sheet preserves the sequence of an edit: rejected frames, tentative crops and the final selection. Compare frames using the same light source before attributing differences to film or processing. Store negatives away from heat and moisture, and keep a description of sleeves and dates separate from assumptions about people in the images.

Field guide

A contact sheet that explains the selection

A small contact sheet can record editorial decisions more honestly than a page of unrelated hero images. Use images you made or may legally reproduce.

Method

  1. Choose six photographs of one ordinary subject and retain the unedited sequence and original timestamps in a private folder.
  2. Create thumbnails in capture order and mark focus, exposure and composition problems without deleting the rejected frames.
  3. Select one frame and write a caption explaining the visual decision; distinguish your interpretation from a factual claim about the scene.
