A star chart

A star chart is useful only when the time and location match.

A star chart encodes a particular location and time. Orient it using directions on the ground, not by rotating it until a constellation looks familiar.

Prepare a chart card with date, time, latitude and cardinal directions, then orient it with a known bright object. Do not assume a static chart fits every night.

The lunar cycle

The lunar cycle changes the illuminated shape visible from Earth.

The lunar phase depends on the relative positions of the Sun, Earth and Moon. It is not caused by Earth's shadow during ordinary monthly changes.

Draw the Sun, Earth and Moon at a few different relative positions to explain phases. Distinguish the ordinary lunar cycle from an eclipse.

03 / Science

Light pollution

Bright city lighting makes faint objects much harder to see.

Light pollution reduces visibility of faint objects and changes with weather. Record approximate viewing conditions alongside your observations.

Compare two hypothetical observing places for glare and horizon obstruction while keeping safe access in view. Do not claim that a darker image proves better conditions.

Simple observation logs

Observation logs should distinguish weather from object movement.

A small observing log needs a date, time, location and instrument notes. A sketch with a few labelled points can be more useful than an unindexed photograph.

Design a simple observation record with visible objects, missing targets and weather notes. Blank entries are more accurate than remembered details added later.

A star chart connects what an observer sees to direction, time and coordinate conventions. A sketch made from memory can still be useful if it is labeled honestly and not presented as a measurement. Before interpreting a faint patch of light, account for haze, local lighting and the limits of the instrument being used.

Field guide

A lunar observation sheet with source labels

A sky reference is useful when its chart-based predictions are clearly separate from what a person actually saw.

Method

  1. Consult a current almanac for your locality and record local time, time zone, moon phase and the date of the source.
  2. Sketch the visible illuminated edge from a safe location, adding notes about clouds and light pollution without inventing surface features.
  3. Compare your sketch with the predicted phase, noting uncertainty in orientation and whether the Moon was actually visible.
