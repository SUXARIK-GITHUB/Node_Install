Reading the wind

Wind direction is read together with the water and the sails.

Wind direction and gusts matter more than a single reported speed. Observe flags and water texture while comparing them with an official marine forecast.

Draw three arrows for true wind, boat direction and apparent wind in a simple thought experiment. Use local training and forecasts before any actual sailing.

02 / Sailing

Chart basics

A chart is a simplified decision tool, not a picture of the sea.

Depth markings and chart symbols use specific datums and conventions. Check the chart edition and local notices before using it for navigation.

Mark depth contours and shallow areas on a sample chart, noting the scale and edition. Never navigate real water from an undated illustration.

03 / Sailing

A simple knot

A well-tied knot still needs to suit its actual load and purpose.

A simple knot must suit its intended load and material. Practice the movements ashore and inspect the finished knot before trusting it.

Practice drawing the sequence of a basic knot and label its working end and load direction. A sketch is not proof a knot is safe for critical use.

04 / Sailing

Weather windows

Weather windows must be checked shortly before any departure.

A weather window includes visibility, waves and the return leg, not only a calm departure. Always use qualified local advice for real voyages.

Create a go-or-stay checklist with wind, visibility, tide and a return option. Write down which conditions would cancel rather than merely delay a trip.

What to look for: A shore-based passage planning card

A nautical chart is a reference with a specific scale and update history, not an assurance that a route is safe today. Learn to distinguish depth soundings, buoys and coastline symbols, then confirm actual conditions from current local sources. Weather windows and access limits matter more than an elegant line drawn across the water.

Field guide

A shore-based passage planning card

Good sailing notes start with decisions that can be checked before departure. This is a desk exercise, not a live routing service or a seaworthiness certificate.

Method

  1. Mark departure, intended destination, safe alternatives and areas restricted to navigation on a current official chart.
  2. Read the latest marine forecast, tidal references and local notices from recognized authorities; write down the time each was checked.
  3. Describe conditions that cancel the journey, including visibility, crew readiness and equipment problems, rather than inventing travel times.
