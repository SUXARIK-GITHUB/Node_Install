Moon sketches

Sketch the Moon at the same hour to notice its changing shape.

A Moon sketch should record the date, approximate time and viewing conditions. Repeating the view across several nights reveals the changing illuminated portion.

Sketch the Moon on two different evenings and label the time, orientation and approximate conditions. Do not fill unseen crater detail from memory.

02 / Astronomy

A sky map

A printed sky chart works without a phone or network connection.

A planisphere or printed star chart can help identify bright constellations without a battery. Check the chart for your latitude and season.

Draw a simple directional sky chart with a few obvious landmarks and check its time and location before identifying a faint object.

03 / Astronomy

Darkness and light

Nearby lighting can erase faint stars from otherwise clear skies.

Nearby streetlights reduce contrast long before the sky looks bright to the eye. Shield your screen and give night vision time to adapt.

Compare two observing spots for direct glare, horizon visibility and safe access. A darker place is not useful if the route home is uncertain.

04 / Astronomy

Observing seasons

A short observing diary makes seasonal changes easier to follow.

Choose a small group of familiar stars to revisit each season. A log of their position and visibility is more helpful than an unlabelled image collection.

Make four seasonal observing cards with different target types and a space for failed sightings. Keep each observation tied to its actual date.

What to look for: A useful log for the first clear night

A sky sketch needs a time, direction and note of local lighting to be useful later. The Moon may be easy to find, while faint objects disappear in city glare. Compare two evenings without assuming that a missing star has moved; clouds, haze and adaptation of the eye are ordinary alternative explanations.

Field guide

A useful log for the first clear night

Observing the sky does not require making up a spectacular discovery. A repeatable notebook gives more value: time, approximate location, sky conditions, visible landmarks and a simple sketch of the field.

Method

  1. Choose a familiar open observing area with legal access and safe footing. Check local sunset, forecast and lunar phase from a reliable current source. A white flashlight can ruin night adaptation; a dim red light may be more comfortable for note-taking.
  2. Write the local date, time and time zone. Draw the shape of an easy-to-recognize star pattern, then mark nearby brighter stars relative to one another. Do not infer an object from a picture without checking a current chart.
  3. Repeat the sketch at a different time and record what moved relative to the horizon. Separate changes in cloud cover from the apparent movement of the sky. Include the equipment used, including whether observations were unaided.

Limits and safety

Do not look at the Sun through binoculars or a telescope without a correctly fitted purpose-built solar filter. Never treat a generic chart as a live sky map.
