Bold hierarchy

Bold hierarchy lets a single message survive a crowded street.

A strong hierarchy makes the first reading point obvious from several steps away. Step back from the poster before judging fine details.

Make three typographic treatments of one original headline using only size, weight and spacing. Compare recognition from across a room and on a phone.

A two-color poster

Two ink colors can create depth through scale and overlap.

Two inks can create rich relationships through overprint and contrast. Test the actual printing process rather than predicting every mixed color on screen.

Construct a poster with two colors and a neutral paper field, checking text contrast rather than assuming a bright accent stays readable.

Letter spacing

Letter spacing changes the perceived weight of a heading.

Letter spacing changes the perceived width of a word and can upset a balanced composition. Judge whole phrases at their final print size.

Print a few lines with intentionally tight and loose tracking, then note where individual letters begin to merge. Keep the source text identical.

Printed texture

Paper grain can become part of the printed image.

Paper texture may soften small lines and type. A single printed proof can reveal more than several revisions on a calibrated display.

Sketch the effect of rough paper and misregistration on a small printed mark. Avoid presenting an on-screen filter as a real print test.

What to look for: A two-color poster hierarchy test

Strong hierarchy is not simply a matter of making one word enormous. Test whether a poster's main idea remains legible at arm's length and as a small image; spacing and contrast may matter more than decoration. Compare the same message in two colors while keeping type and placement stable, so the effect is interpretable.

Field guide

A two-color poster hierarchy test

A poster can be legible without relying on decoration. An exercise with one headline, one fact and a date reveals the effect of scale.

Method

  1. Write a fictitious event clearly labelled as a layout exercise and arrange its title, date and location as plain text.
  2. Produce two layouts that keep the same wording but change size and alignment; check their reading order at thumbnail size.
  3. Test both at high zoom and in monochrome so the information remains understandable without color cues.

Limits and safety

Do not present a sample event as a real public announcement. Respect image and font licensing when producing a finished poster.
