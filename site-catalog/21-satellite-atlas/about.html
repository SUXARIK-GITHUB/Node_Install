Four paths for further study

Choose the subject most relevant to your question. Each item leads to an article and a practical exercise.

  1. Reading a raster

    Print a tiny grid of raster cells and mark the ground area each cell stands for. Explain why zooming the display cannot recover features absent from the source image.

  2. Cloud coverage

    Compare two imagined satellite passes with different cloud masks and note which parts of a landscape are actually visible. Keep hidden regions labelled as unknown.

  3. Ground sampling

    Place a small road and a large lake on sketches with different sampling distances. Decide which shape remains interpretable at each scale without fabricating detail.

  4. Map projections

    Trace two map projections on a simple globe outline, noting where distances or shapes stretch. Match each projection to the question the map is supposed to answer.

Method and limits

The following is an independent reading exercise, not a report of fieldwork.

A raster cell represents a ground footprint. A picture displayed at two hundred percent zoom still contains the same source measurements; it does not gain new streets or tree crowns. Check the stated pixel size and sensor characteristics before interpreting any small object.

For a classroom exercise, sketch a coastline on squared paper, then redraw it using larger cells. Notice which narrow features disappear. This illustrates resolution loss without claiming that the drawing is an actual satellite observation.

A satellite image is an observation tied to time, resolution and a particular sensor. Brightness alone rarely establishes what a surface contains. Compare two scenes only after checking cloud cover, spatial scale and coordinate reference; a difference in color may reflect the imaging method rather than a real change on the ground.
